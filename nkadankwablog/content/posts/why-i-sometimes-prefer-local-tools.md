I sometimes prefer a tool that runs on my own computer for three connected reasons: privacy, access, and ease of use.

## Keeping the work closer

A local tool can process a file without uploading it to a service. That does not automatically make it private—the application may still include telemetry, extensions, or online features—but it gives me a clearer starting boundary. For personal notes, project data, or experiments with local AI, knowing where the data is processed matters.

## Access that does not depend on a service

Local software can remain useful when the internet is slow, an account is unavailable, or a company changes its plan. Files stored in open or well-documented formats are easier to back up and move. I am less comfortable when access to my own work depends entirely on a login and the continued existence of someone else's server.

## Sometimes local is simply easier

Opening an application and working with a nearby file can be faster than uploading, waiting, and downloading again. Integration with the file system, keyboard shortcuts, and other desktop tools also makes repeated tasks feel more direct.

There are trade-offs. I have to install updates, manage storage, create backups, and accept that my laptop may be less powerful than a cloud service. Collaboration is often easier online, and some services provide infrastructure I could not reasonably reproduce.

That is why my preference is “sometimes.” I choose local tools when control, offline access, or a short path to my files matters more than instant sharing or remote scale. The goal is to avoid sending every task to the cloud by default.
