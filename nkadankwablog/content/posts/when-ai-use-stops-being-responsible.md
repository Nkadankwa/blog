A bank statement contains a small history of someone's life. Rent, wages, debt payments, a clinic visit, money sent to a relative. Uploading the whole thing to answer a budgeting question hands over much more than the question requires.

That was one of the examples behind my AI dos and don'ts guides. I wanted rules specific enough to use while a document is still sitting beside the upload button. “Use AI responsibly” leaves an enormous amount for the person reading it to figure out.

I made two editions: an adult guide organised around everyday situations, and a separate guide for teenagers. Working through those situations brings responsibility down to individual actions. Someone chooses the attachment. Someone accepts the answer. Someone presses send.

## What went into the prompt?

Consider a request to organise monthly spending. Category totals may be enough: income, housing, food, transport. A complete statement can also reveal account details and transactions involving other people. Those details have entered the conversation before the model has produced a single word.

The adult guide opens with “Share the question before the statement.” The wording is deliberate. Starting with the question makes it easier to notice how little of the original document the task may need.

A private conversation presents another difficulty. Possessing a copy of a friend's message does not settle whether I should upload it for analysis. The friend chose an audience when they sent it. An instruction to summarise the message changes that audience without asking them.

I find this part of AI use gets buried beneath discussion of the answer. We examine whether the summary is accurate or the advice is sensible. The person whose information supplied the prompt may never know that either exists.

Removing a name can leave a workplace, a date, an unusual event and enough context to identify someone. Privacy requires looking at the actual material. A reassuring label on a service cannot make that decision for me.

## The reference that nobody opened

In June 2023, a US federal judge imposed a $5,000 sanction on two lawyers and their firm after fictitious cases generated by ChatGPT appeared in a court filing. The judge also criticised their conduct after the cases were challenged. [Associated Press's report](https://www.news4jax.com/tech/2023/06/22/lawyers-submitted-bogus-case-law-created-by-chatgpt-a-judge-fined-them-5000/) describes a failure that continued beyond the original generated answer.

I keep returning to the reference list. A citation occupies the place where evidence should be. It has a title, perhaps a date, perhaps a convincing quotation. Until somebody opens the source, its appearance can do the work of authority.

That is why the guides place sources beside the relevant rules. Readers should be able to inspect the basis of a claim without hunting through a disconnected bibliography. A news report about one incident also needs to stay attached to that incident; it cannot establish how every AI service behaves.

For a blog like mine, the uncomfortable part is ordinary. A fluent paragraph can move straight into a draft. Once it fits the surrounding prose, deleting it becomes an inconvenience. Checking may mean losing the sentence, rebuilding the argument or admitting that there is much less evidence than the draft suggested.

Publishing it anyway passes that inconvenience to the reader.

## A finished assignment can conceal an unfinished education

A student can submit a complete explanation while remaining unable to reproduce its reasoning. The document gives the teacher something to mark. It gives the student very little to use when the next problem changes.

The teen guide includes a small exercise: ask for one hint, attempt the next step, then close the tool and explain the method. The point of closing it is practical. An answer can feel familiar while it remains on the screen. Explaining it without that support exposes the missing pieces.

I think about the same gap in programming. A generated change might solve the visible error while leaving its author unable to explain the permissions it needs, the files it touches or what happens when the input is empty. The successful run answers one question. Plenty remain.

Calling the work finished can hide those questions from everyone else. A teammate inherits the code. A teacher assumes the concept has been learned. The next task arrives with the expectation that the previous one built some understanding.

## Someone else's face, someone else's voice

An imitation can borrow the trust attached to a person. In its [March 2023 warning about family emergency scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/03/scammers-use-ai-enhance-their-family-emergency-schemes), the US Federal Trade Commission described how voice cloning could make a fraudulent request sound like a relative. Its advice was to contact that person through a number already known to be theirs.

The guide uses that warning for a concrete rule: verify the person before moving money. Listening harder to the same recording is a poor substitute for making independent contact.

The use of another person's likeness also deserves attention before anything is shared. Imagine an embarrassing image of a classmate circulating in a group chat. That is a hypothetical example in the teen guide, not a reported incident. The person pictured had no part in creating it and now has to deal with an audience.

“It was AI” explains how the image was made. It gives the person targeted very little reason to feel better about what happened.

## The teenager reading the rule may already need help

Writing for teenagers changes the language I want to use. An adult guide can discuss judgement, permissions and the limits of evidence at length. A teenager facing an upsetting chat needs a sentence they can act on: stop replying, close it, tell a trusted adult.

[UNICEF USA's guidance on children and AI](https://www.unicefusa.org/stories/protecting-children-age-ai) informed this emphasis on privacy, human support and conversations about what children encounter online. In the teen edition, I wanted the route to help to remain visible throughout the book.

A rule that only says “never share that” stops too early. The reader may already have shared it. They may be afraid that asking for help will mean punishment, embarrassment or losing access to the device. The guide explicitly tells them to ask another trusted adult if the first person does not help.

That sentence matters to me more than a clever prompt. It leaves a way forward after the mistake.

## The two guides

The [adult and teen PDFs are available here]({{< relref "/guides/ai-guides.md" >}}). The adult edition has 14 rules across personal life, work and everyday use, with dos, don'ts, evidence and practical suggestions. The teen edition has 12 rules, fictional situations to practise with and a safety plan. Both have clickable contents and sources beside the rules they support.

I kept the editions separate because the person reading them changes what needs explaining. An employee deciding whether to upload a work document and a teenager receiving an unsettling message have different decisions in front of them.

The bank statement is still the example I would start with. Before there is an answer to judge, there is a file full of details the question may never have needed.
