Sam Altman’s recent comments about personal computing raise a broader question about who should own the resources behind AI. As reported by Tom’s Hardware from his Cheeky Pint appearance, he argued that scarce memory sitting largely unused in individual computers represents an inefficient allocation of resources. From a data-centre operator’s perspective, the reasoning is clear: shared hardware can serve many customers rather than remain available to a single owner. [Reporting on Altman’s remarks](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/data-centers/openai-ceo-says-its-quite-terrible-for-your-pc-to-have-ram-sam-altman-says-data-centers-offer-better-utilization)

His argument concerned hardware utilisation, rather than whether ordinary people are wasting AI on unimportant requests. Nevertheless, it exposes a tension in the future being offered to consumers. As more computing moves into infrastructure owned by a small number of providers, those companies gain greater influence over what people can access, how much they pay and which conditions apply.

A personal computer may sit idle overnight, but it remains available when its owner returns. Its value includes the freedom to experiment, learn and begin another project without checking a subscription allowance. My interest in running services myself comes partly from that independence. Maintaining a system takes effort, but it also provides control over how that system operates.

Unused capacity can therefore serve a purpose. It is capacity reserved for its owner, ready when needed. An assessment based entirely on continuous utilisation overlooks that benefit.

## Efficiency does not settle the question of ownership

Centralised computing has clear advantages. A data centre can support workloads that would be impractical on a personal machine, while a hosted service can spare users the expense and maintenance of specialised hardware. For many people, paying for access is a reasonable choice.

My experience with a homelab has made the responsibilities of ownership clear. Installing services, maintaining access and troubleshooting failures all take time. Local AI brings similar demands, including suitable hardware, electricity and ongoing software maintenance.

The convenience of cloud services is therefore worth recognising. However, convenience alone does not determine how much computing should move into systems controlled by providers. That decision also affects privacy, competition and the ability to continue working when a service changes.

Altman’s commercial position is relevant here. Someone building a business around shared computing has an interest in greater demand for that infrastructure. His argument can be technically reasonable while still reflecting priorities that differ from those of an individual computer owner.

The question is whether the efficiency gained through consolidation produces enough benefit for consumers to justify the control they give up.

## Can consumer subscriptions sustain the promised level of access?

Consumer AI pricing presents a related difficulty. A monthly subscription offers the customer a predictable charge, but the cost of serving that customer can vary considerably. Occasional requests for explanations and repeated agent tasks involving research, coding and tool use place different demands on the service.

ChatGPT’s pricing page already distinguishes plans through features and usage allowances, including several Pro usage tiers. The relevant comparison is the offer currently available to each customer, since prices and entitlements can change. [ChatGPT plans and pricing](https://chatgpt.com/pricing/)

Whether those payments can sustain the level of work users are encouraged to expect remains an important question.

Large infrastructure investments do not, on their own, prove that consumer subscriptions are unsustainable. Establishing the economics of a particular plan would require information about actual usage, serving costs, operating expenses and how the company allocates its wider investments. The subscription price alone cannot provide that answer.

Consumers can still reasonably question how durable the current arrangement will be. Products increasingly promise to carry out longer tasks, use more tools and work with less supervision. If customers adopt those capabilities, providers must support the resulting demand through greater efficiency, additional revenue, lower margins or limits on consumption.

My concern is the growing expectation that people should depend on these services before the long-term terms of access are clear.

## Lower costs do not guarantee a lower bill

There is a credible case for optimism. Improvements in hardware and model efficiency could make useful AI progressively cheaper to provide. OpenAI has presented model-price reductions as part of its strategy for expanding access, and those improvements deserve consideration. [OpenAI’s account of building abundant intelligence](https://openai.com/index/building-abundant-intelligence/)

However, the cost of an individual operation and the cost of completing a task are different measures. An agent may perform many steps to produce a result that previously required a single response. Total consumption can therefore rise even while each operation becomes cheaper.

This creates a difficult balance for consumer products. Their value grows as they take on more work, but sustained use may require a higher subscription tier, additional credits or closer management of allowances.

A business may justify that expenditure against revenue or labour costs. An individual often has less flexibility. A student, hobbyist or person seeking work can benefit substantially from AI without having a budget that reflects the value they receive.

If access to the most capable systems increasingly depends on the ability to pay, the promise of broadly available intelligence becomes less convincing. An affordable plan needs to provide enough access for meaningful work, with conditions that customers can understand before relying on it.

## Personal computing preserves an alternative

Ownership belongs in this discussion because it gives consumers another way to work. Local tools may be less capable than leading hosted systems, but they can remain useful when a subscription becomes too expensive, restrictive or unsuitable.

That alternative deserves protection. Treating consumer hardware primarily as an inefficient allocation of resources risks overlooking the independence it provides. A market with several cloud providers can offer competition while still leaving users dependent on continued rental payments.

The future I would prefer allows hosted AI and personal computing to develop alongside each other. Some tasks will justify powerful remote infrastructure, while others should remain practical on hardware people own. Moving work between those options should be straightforward enough for the choice to remain meaningful.

Altman’s concern about utilisation is understandable within the economics of a data centre. It provides only part of the basis for deciding how individuals should access computing.

The AI industry is asking people to make its products part of their education, employment and daily routines. That request should come with a clearer account of what dependable access will cost and how consumers will retain control over their work. Until those terms are clearer, an idle personal computer still offers something valuable: capacity its owner can use without negotiating another subscription.
