My first real step toward a home lab was not buying hardware. It was slowing down long enough to ask what I wanted a home lab to do for me.

I had a mix of interests—files, notes, self-hosting, Linux, automation—and I wanted a place where those experiments could live together. That made the research stage essential. A home lab can be a lot of things: a quiet place to learn, a server for useful services, a testing ground for new tools, or a way to understand the systems that usually stay invisible.

## Starting with the right questions

Before choosing equipment, I explored beginner guides, community discussions, and home-lab videos. I wanted to understand the recurring building blocks: hardware, operating systems, storage, networking, containers, and backups. I also wanted to avoid buying into someone else’s ideal setup before knowing what would fit my own budget and goals.

The useful question became: *what can I learn with the resources I have now?* That shift made the project feel possible.

## The direction I chose

My early priorities were straightforward:

- Learn the Linux command line and filesystem.
- Understand SSH, networking, and remote access.
- Experiment with Docker and Docker Compose.
- Build toward simple services such as file sharing, backups, and monitoring.

I am treating the home lab as a gradual project rather than a shopping list. The aim is to make each layer understandable before putting the next one on top.

## Research that helped

- Official documentation for Linux distributions, Docker, and the tools I test
- YouTube channels such as Techno Tim, LearnLinuxTV, and NetworkChuck for broad concepts
- r/homelab and r/linux4noobs for practical conversations and real-world trade-offs
- Discord communities where people share working setups as well as the mistakes behind them

## A foundation, not a finish line

There is no single correct way to start a home lab. The best first step is the one that lets you learn consistently. This blog is where I keep the research, the experiments, the false starts, and the small wins while they are still real—not only after they have been cleaned up into a success story.
