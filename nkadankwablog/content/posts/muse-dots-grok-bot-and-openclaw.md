For a long time, making a computer do something repeatedly meant telling it exactly what to do. A scheduled job ran a script. An email rule matched a condition. A workflow connected one application's event to another application's action. Setting it up required effort, but the instruction was usually narrow enough to inspect.

The new personal agents offer a much broader arrangement. Describe an outcome, give the assistant access, and let it work out the steps. That changes the relationship between a person and their software. We are being asked to delegate judgement as well as execution.

I have not used Muse, dots, Grok Bot or OpenClaw. This is an editorial based on their public documentation, checked on 14 October 2026. I am interested in the bargain they offer; I cannot say which actually finishes a difficult week of work with the fewest mistakes.

## Four products, two arrangements

Meta describes Muse as a personal agent with a dedicated cloud computer and browser. OpenAI's dots also have a cloud computer and can pursue ongoing work. Grok Bot presents several cooperating Bots that use applications and continue working in the background. These are the providers' descriptions, rather than results I have independently verified. [Muse announcement](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/09/introducing-muse-personal-ai-agent/), [official dots documentation](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/dots), [Grok Bot overview](https://x.ai/bot).

OpenClaw offers another arrangement: an open-source assistant that runs on a machine you control, supports different model providers and keeps its state there. Its own description includes messaging channels, persistent memory and tools for acting on a computer. [OpenClaw overview](https://openclaw.ai/).

The meaningful comparison begins with who operates that environment. With a managed agent, a company makes many of the infrastructure decisions. With OpenClaw, the person running it has more decisions to make and more responsibility for their consequences.

That distinction reminds me of the move from applications on a personal computer to services accessed through a browser. We gained easier access and less maintenance. We also grew accustomed to another company deciding how long a feature existed, which plan included it and whether our account could use it.

Persistent agents could deepen that dependency. An assistant may accumulate the working history of projects, preferences and recurring responsibilities. Moving away could mean reconstructing an arrangement that has gradually become part of how someone manages their day.

## Convenience deserves a serious hearing

It is easy for someone interested in self-hosting to underestimate the value of having somebody else run the machinery. Most people want a finished report or a reminder that arrives on time. They have little reason to enjoy maintaining the service that produces it.

If a managed agent removes enough setup and maintenance, that is useful work the provider has done. An open-source alternative does not make the user's time free. Spending an evening repairing an assistant can consume the time it was meant to save.

I can also see why these products are presented differently. Muse's personal-assistant framing invites a place in everyday life. Dots fits ongoing work inside ChatGPT. Grok Bot's team framing encourages people to divide responsibilities among named agents. Those descriptions shape how much work a person may feel comfortable handing over, before reliability has even been established.

Calling something a teammate makes delegation feel familiar. A software subscription, however, does not carry the same relationship or accountability as employing a person. I would want the product to make its actual permissions at least as visible as its personality.

## OpenClaw keeps an alternative available

What interests me about OpenClaw is the possibility of changing the surrounding system: choosing a model, inspecting configuration and deciding where the assistant's state lives. That gives people a route to adapting the machinery around their work.

It also gives them machinery to maintain. Self-hosting does not establish that a system is secure, and using a remote model can still send information outside the machine. OpenClaw's security documentation discusses trust boundaries, permissions and the risks of giving an agent tools. [OpenClaw security guidance](https://docs.openclaw.ai/gateway/security).

The freedom matters even to people who never install it. A credible open alternative puts pressure on managed services to earn their convenience. It keeps the idea of a personal assistant from becoming synonymous with renting a permanent place inside one company's ecosystem.

I would judge this category by how well a user can inspect work, narrow access and leave. A polished introduction matters for an afternoon. Exportable work, understandable permissions and a workable exit matter after a year.

Muse, dots and Grok Bot may make delegated computing accessible to many more people. OpenClaw offers a way to participate while retaining more control over the system. Both directions deserve scrutiny. Before an assistant becomes responsible for an increasing share of my day, I want to know how easily I can take the keys back.
