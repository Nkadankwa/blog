I do not like seeing useful things go to waste. That is why repairability affects how long I can keep something I already own.

My previous Android phone tested that belief. Before I seriously considered replacing it, I changed the power button, repaired the battery connectors, and reglued the screen when it lifted—about three times. None of those repairs made the phone new just made it last longer.

## Repair changes the replacement question

When a single part fails, the whole device should not automatically become rubbish. A replaceable battery, accessible connector, available part, or understandable screw can turn a failure into a job. Glue, paired components, unavailable manuals, and expensive assemblies can turn the same failure into a replacement purchase.

Repairability does not mean every repair is sensible. There is a point where repeated failures, safety concerns, poor software support, or the price of parts make replacement reasonable. My old phone eventually reached that point. The important thing is that I had a choice before then.

## The value is also in understanding the object

Opening a device made its problems less mysterious. A broken power button became a component and a connection rather than a vague feeling that the phone was dying.

I care about performance and price, but I also want to know whether an ordinary failure can be fixed. A device that lasts longer does not only save money. It reduces waste and respects the materials and work already inside it.
