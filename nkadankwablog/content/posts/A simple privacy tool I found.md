My email address has become one of those pieces of information I hand over almost automatically. A shop wants it before showing me a receipt. A website wants it before letting me download something. A service I am only testing wants it before I have decided whether I trust the service at all.

For a long time, I treated that as the normal cost of using the internet. I would enter the same address, untick the marketing box if I noticed it, and hope the inbox did not become more crowded. The problem is that an email address is not just a place where messages arrive. It is also a permanent identifier that can connect accounts created years apart.

That is why Firefox Relay caught my attention.

## A boundary instead of another inbox

Relay creates an email mask that forwards messages to my actual inbox. The website gets the mask, while I still receive the message without opening and maintaining another email account.

The idea is simple, but it changes the balance of the sign-up form. Instead of deciding whether a random website deserves my main address, I can give it an address with a boundary around it. If the messages become unwanted, or the address appears somewhere it should not, I can disable that mask without disturbing the accounts and people that use my real address.

What I like is not the promise of disappearing online. Relay does not make that promise. It gives me a smaller and more believable kind of control.

## Privacy tools have to survive ordinary life

I have noticed that privacy advice often sounds reasonable until it adds five more steps to something I need to do quickly. A tool may be powerful, but if using it means leaving the page, opening another app, creating something manually, and recording several details, convenience usually wins.

Relay's browser integration matters for that reason. Creating or selecting a mask can happen where I am already entering the address. The privacy decision becomes part of the normal action instead of a separate task I need to remember later.

That does not mean I need a different mask for every interaction. The tool makes the most sense to me for newsletters, shops, downloads, trials, and accounts I am still evaluating. These are situations where a website needs a way to reach me but does not necessarily need the address tied to the rest of my online life.

## The control comes with another dependency

An email mask is not anonymity. A company may still recognise me through a phone number, payment information, delivery address, browser data, or the details I provide. Relay also becomes part of the chain between an account and my inbox. If I lose access to it or forget which mask belongs to which service, account recovery could become more complicated.

That means the masks still need organisation. Important accounts need unique passwords, two-factor authentication, and a record in my password manager. Relay reduces the spread of one identifier; it does not replace the rest of account security.

## A small tool with an honest purpose

The privacy tools I trust most are not the ones that promise to make me invisible. They are the ones that solve one clear problem and are honest about where their usefulness ends.

Firefox Relay cannot stop every company from collecting information about me. What it can do is challenge the assumption that my permanent email address belongs in every form. That is a modest change, but it gives me a moment to ask a better question before signing up: does this website really need to know the address I use everywhere else?

More often than I realised, the answer is no.
